## 1. Postmortem

### What we tried vs. what we shipped

Tried: a learning/demo FPS that looks polished and plays engagingly. Shipped: everything planned — v1 arena (11 units), combat feel + lighting, rooms-and-corridors map with real bot navigation and honest sensing, minimap + room accents, armory loop (MG + grenades as pickups), killfeed, and a visual-fidelity pass (textures, decals, viewmodel pass, skybox, post-processing) — plus a 32-unit review-findings cleanup. The gap is not in delivery; it is in the ten shipped-then-fixed bugs along the way, which cluster into three repeating shapes (see Root Causes).

### Dimension Ratings

| Dimension | Rating | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Scope & Requirements | Strong | Every feature went felt-experience → brainstorm → verified requirements; bundling attempts were split into coherent units before planning. |
| Planning & Sequencing | Strong− | Spike-first and phase-gating held throughout; the one gap was not planning *across* concurrent autonomous builds until two nearly collided in one working tree. |
| Specification Quality | Good | Three author-level spec errors (fire cadence "existing knobs", decal "sim raycast unlock", "verified" 60fps) — all caught by the independent verifier/flow-analysis layers before build, none by self-review. |
| Technical Execution | Good | Clean pure-sim architecture and 44 test files, but 6 documented logic bugs + 4 post-build render fixes shipped first and were fixed after. |
| Language & Platform Fit | Good | Three.js + Rapier fit well; the Command seam is the standout. Platform traps (layer-light semantics, composer tone-mapping order, background-ignores-fog, texture color space) were all *known* pre-build and two still bit. |
| Collaboration & Communication | Strong | Opening every cycle with played experience beat the candidate shelf every time; visual sketches resolved shape decisions in one round; corrections stayed one message cheap. |
| Recovery | Strong | Every bug got root cause + solutions doc + regression test; two fixes (armed-latch, vector-rejection) became reusable guardrails later cycles cited by name. |
| Anatomy Gaps | Mixed | Four structural gaps produced nearly all defects: basis/handedness math, lifecycle state ownership, cross-producer event wiring, render-pass state. |

### Root Causes

1. **Convention-crossing math applied by pattern, not derived.** Three independent handedness/sign bugs (strafe basis, obstacle-avoidance blend, minimap rotation) — all magnitude-preserving, all invisible to review and to invariant tests. The math was "plausible-looking"; nothing forced a directional proof until the third incident made it doctrine.
2. **Lifecycle contracts were implicit.** Three bugs of one shape (retreat survives death, killfeed survives restart, grenade kill bypasses the MG death-strip): new stateful systems had to *opt in* to death/reset boundaries and nothing enforced registration. Fixed culturally late (CONCEPTS' Match Reset entry) rather than structurally early.
3. **Named risks stayed prose.** The visual pass's research explicitly flagged depth-clear and layer-lighting traps; both still shipped broken and were fixed post-build. A risk written in a plan's Risks section retired nothing — only risks converted into a test scenario or a named play-check did.

## 2. Structural Lessons

**Structural density:** High

**L1 — A pure, command-driven simulation core is the enabling decision of the whole project.**
What happened: v1 defined one Command shape for player, bots, and hypothetical remote peers, and kept `src/sim/` renderer-free (guard-tested). Every later feature — FSM bots, navigation, honest sensing, headless tests, the still-open multiplayer door — got cheaper because of it.
Why it matters: the boundary is what made 44 test files possible in a game. Transfers to any domain: separate the deterministic core from I/O and every consumer becomes swappable.
Dig deeper: game-loop/timestep architecture, ports-and-adapters.

**L2 — Magnitude-preserving errors demand direction-asserting tests.**
What happened: three separate basis/handedness bugs passed every length/NaN/consistency check; only sign assertions (`dot(result, desired) > 0`, "ahead projects to negative y") caught them.
Why it matters: rotation/basis code has a failure mode invisible to the tests people naturally write. Transfers to graphics, robotics, geo, physics — anywhere two coordinate conventions meet.
Previously flagged? Became explicit doctrine only after incident #3 (minimap).

**L3 — State surviving a boundary it shouldn't is a bug *class*; design the boundary, not the fix.**
What happened: retreat timers survived death (absolute deadline vs frozen per-entity clock); killfeed survived restart (unregistered reset hook); a second kill path bypassed a death effect (branch-gated wiring). The durable fixes were structural: countdown timers decremented by always-running loops, an explicit reset-hook registry, effects subscribed at shared emission points.
Why it matters: transfers to sessions, caches, sagas, any system with lifecycle boundaries. The question to ask at design time: "which boundaries does this state cross, and who clears it at each?"

**L4 — Independent verification catches what self-review structurally cannot.**
What happened: every brainstorm's checkable claims went to a fresh-context verifier; three author errors were refuted cheaply before any code existed. The author who wrote a claim never once caught their own claim being wrong.
Why it matters: anchoring is not a discipline problem, it's an architecture problem — solved by separate contexts, not by trying harder.

**L5 — A named risk is inert until it becomes a gate.**
What happened: pre-build research documented the exact two render-pass traps that later shipped broken. The plans that converted findings into test scenarios (decal orientation, reset regression) shipped those parts clean; the risks left as prose shipped broken.
Why it matters: risk registers without owning gates are decoration, in any engineering process.

**L6 — Felt experience out-selects feature lists.**
What happened: all five cycles opened by asking what playing actually felt like; the answers ("bots in a box", "all walls look same", "gun through wall, walls bland") produced better scopes than the accumulated candidate shelf ever would have — wayfinding wasn't even on the shelf.
Why it matters: backlog items are hypotheses; lived friction is evidence.

### Recurring Structural Gaps

1. **Basis/handedness composition** — 3 incidents. Highest priority; the codebase now has the reference technique (vector rejection) and test shape, but the underlying model (why right-handed bases compose the way they do) is patched, not owned.
2. **Lifecycle state ownership** — 3 incidents, now codified in CONCEPTS but worth owning as a design instinct rather than a checklist.
3. **Render-pass/pipeline state** — 2 shipped fixes + 2 more near-misses; newest and least internalized.

### Missed Opportunities

None material — density was high because the project documented its own failures unusually well (six solutions docs with root causes and prevention sections did half this report's work).

**Study prompt — Topic 1 (paste into a fresh session):**
> In a Three.js + Rapier FPS I shipped three separate handedness/sign bugs: a strafe `right` vector that was a valid perpendicular but wrong-handed for the rendered camera (which applies a +π yaw correction); an obstacle-avoidance formula `normalize(desired + normal*k)` that reversed movers for near-head-on hits; and a minimap rotation that composed a world-to-screen z-flip with the wrong sign. All three passed magnitude/orthogonality tests. Teach me: (1) the core model of bases, handedness, and rotation composition that makes these mistakes impossible to write; (2) the standard failure modes when two conventions meet (sim vs camera, world vs screen); (3) how the same class appears in robotics/OpenGL/Unity; (4) a mental model or checklist for deriving (not pattern-matching) any basis-crossing formula; (5) canonical resources.

**Study prompt — Topic 2:**
> Same project, three bugs of one shape: a retreat timer stored as `tick + duration` against a per-entity clock that freezes while dead; a killfeed that survived match restart because it never registered with the reset step; a death-effect that ran only in the hitscan branch and missed grenade kills. Teach me the structural theory of lifecycle boundaries: (1) state ownership and who clears what at death/pause/reset/reconnect; (2) why countdown-remaining ticked by an always-running system beats absolute deadlines; (3) registry-based teardown vs implicit cleanup; (4) how this class appears in web sessions, caches, and distributed sagas; (5) canonical resources.

**Study prompt — Topic 3:**
> Same project: a viewmodel depth-clear pass erased world geometry, and moving the weapon to its own render layer silently stopped its muzzle light from lighting the world — both risks were *documented in the plan* and still shipped. Teach me the GPU-pipeline state model: (1) what state persists across draw calls and passes (depth, stencil, color space, tone mapping); (2) how layer/visibility systems interact with lights in scene-graph renderers; (3) the standard multi-pass patterns (depth clear, render targets, composers) and their failure modes; (4) a mental model for reasoning about "what does pass N inherit from pass N−1"; (5) canonical resources.

## Cumulative Model Update

**Before:** bugs in a game project would live in the hard algorithms — pathfinding, ballistics, AI.
**After:** the algorithms shipped clean (A*, grenade arcs, FSMs — pure, test-first, zero incidents); every significant defect lived where two *conventions* crossed a boundary: sim yaw vs camera yaw, entity clock vs world clock, one event producer vs two, pass N's GPU state vs pass N+1's. Correctness there is relational, not local — it cannot be seen in one file, one test, or one review.
**Because:** ten shipped bugs, zero in pure algorithm cores, ten at boundaries.

## Next Session: One Thing To Do Differently

**Every risk written into a plan must name the gate that retires it — a test scenario or a named play-check in a specific unit — or it doesn't go in the Risks section at all.** The two most expensive post-build fixes (depth-clear, layer lighting) were both *known* risks that stayed prose; the risks that were converted into gates (decal orientation sign test, reset regression) shipped clean on the first try. This is one editing rule at plan-write time, and it directly attacks the project's proven failure mode.
