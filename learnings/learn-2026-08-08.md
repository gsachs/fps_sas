## 1. Postmortem

### What we tried vs. what we shipped

Since the last pass: one large feature (the asymmetric-districts arena, six districts replacing the symmetric four-corner map, plus an MG-only loadout), then a review-and-repair arc that ran considerably longer than the feature itself. Everything planned shipped. The suite grew 443 → 510 tests, and 156 commits are green and pushed.

The gap worth studying is not delivery — it is **what the pipeline could not see**. The districts feature merged with a full plan, nine layout invariant tests, a verification contract, human play validation, and 443 passing tests. It shipped an arena that was **not a closed volume**: a flood fill from outside the floor reached all twelve spawn points. Nothing in the suite could fail on that, and the plan had assigned it to a human checkpoint ("no visible seams or holes") that a person walking around did not hit.

That is the shape of the whole arc. Thirteen distinct defects were found and fixed this session. **Zero were caught by the test suite before the fact.**

|---|---|---|
| Multi-agent code review + numerical verification | 6 | geometry, logic, stale data, dead assets |
| The human playing the game | 8 | every single visual-fidelity defect |
| Me, mid-implementation | 4 | wiring near-misses, a broken debug hook |

### Root Causes

1. **Gates asserted properties of parts; the defects were properties of the whole.** `layout.test.js` had nine invariant tests — every doorway wide enough, every district's nav envelope clear, every corridor pair convex, every sightline terminating. Not one asked whether the union of walls *bounds a region*. Same shape three more times: the shadow fix derived the box extent but not the near plane or light distance (one axis of a four-axis containment property); no test asked whether two walls occupy the same space (z-fighting); no test asked whether the crosshair and the shot point the same way (recoil).
2. **Nothing in the pipeline can see pixels.** Eight of thirteen defects were purely "what it looks like" — peter-panning seams, z-fighting, boxy decals, faceted flashes. That category has *zero* automated coverage, so its entire failure surface is discovered by a human, late, and every fix ships unguarded against recurrence. I built a headless-Chromium measurement rig **three separate times** this session and discarded it each time.
3. **three.js constants whose units are implicit.** `shadow.bias` is normalized by the shadow camera's depth span, so the same literal meant 0.053 world units at the old arena size and 0.110 after I moved the sun — I doubled a pre-existing artifact while fixing a different one. Same family: `shadowSide` silently defaults to *back* faces; `MeshStandardMaterial.color` multiplies `map`. These are not knowledge gaps about rendering, they are gaps about *this library's defaults*.

## 2. Structural Lessons

**Structural density:** High

**L7 — Enumerating properties of the parts never establishes a property of the whole.**
What happened: nine passing invariant tests over walls, doorways and districts, and the arena was open to the void at three corners. The fix that mattered was not a tenth part-property; it was a flood fill over the *assembled* geometry — one assertion about the union.
Why it matters: this is the most transferable lesson of the pass. "Every record validates" is not "the ledger balances"; "every endpoint is authenticated" is not "there is no unauthenticated path". Whenever a system is assembled from independently-valid pieces, the interesting invariants are closure properties, and they must be computed over the assembly.
Previously flagged? No — new, and immediately the highest-yield gap (4 incidents in one session).

**L8 — A constant whose units are defined by another value is a time bomb; bound the derived quantity, not the literal.**
What happened: `shadow.bias = -0.0006` is scaled by `far - near`. That span went 89 → 133 → 183 across three arena changes, so a value tuned once quietly grew to twice its intended world-space size and lifted every wall's shadow off its base. The durable guard asserts `|bias| * (far - near) < 0.02` — the thing that actually matters — not the literal.
Why it matters: any normalized, relative or percentage-of-something constant has this property. Transfers to timeouts expressed as fractions of a budget, thresholds relative to a window size, tolerances scaled by magnitude.

**L9 — An optional call on a required collaborator converts a wiring bug into silent behaviour loss.**
What happened: I wrote `combat.tickAirdrops?.(entityAccessor)`. It immediately hid four test combat stacks wired without it — the descent silently did nothing in all of them and every test passed. Removing the `?.` surfaced all four at once. Separately, `__debugTracerCount` had been counting `type === 'Line'` and returning zero ever since tracers stopped being Lines.
Why it matters: this project's signature failure mode is the silent no-op, now with five instances across two reports (shadow extent, `?.`, tracer count, shadow bias, `shadowSide`). Optional chaining on a collaborator that is *not* optional is the cheapest way to manufacture one.
Previously flagged? The class was — as "named risks stay prose" and layer-lighting. This is its sharpest, most mechanical form.

**L10 — Verify the guard fails before you trust it.**
What happened: for every regression test added this session, I reverted the fix and confirmed the test failed — the enclosure test, the pillar-overlap test, the shadow-frustum test, the bias bound, the z-fight guard, the placeholder-disposal guard. Two of them would otherwise have been decorative: the raggedness test passed at a sampling resolution too coarse to see the property it claimed to check.
Why it matters: an unverified regression test is a claim, not a guard, and it is indistinguishable from a real one at review time.

**L11 — For anything with a number, measure instead of inspecting.**
What happened: the wall seam was settled by sampling one column of the framebuffer (junction 76 luminance against floor 47, dropping to 49 after the fix), not by staring. Enclosure by flood fill. Shadow clipping by projecting all 82 caster corners into light space. Recoil by comparing camera-forward against shot-direction (−0.089 vs 0.000). In every case the measurement contradicted at least one confident verbal claim — including one reviewer's assertion that the shadow frustum "does hold".
Why it matters: rendering and geometry look like domains for judgement, and they are almost entirely domains for arithmetic.

### Recurring Structural Gaps

1. **Render-pipeline and library semantics** — 6+ incidents across both reports, now the clear top source. Promoted to Phase 1 of the study plan.
2. **Whole-system invariants** — 4 incidents, all this session, all invisible to a green 500-test suite. New, and the highest-yield thing to learn.
3. **Rendered-output blindness** — not a knowledge gap but a capability gap; 8 of 13 defects live here with no observer.
4. **Lifecycle state ownership** — *improving*. New `airdropping` state was given its boundaries at design time (cleared on death mid-drop, on parking, on match reset) with no incident. The 08-06 lesson landed.
5. **Basis/handedness** — *dormant*. Zero incidents since 08-06, was three. Demoted to reference.

I wrote it three times — a bias sweep with luminance profiling, a before/after z-fighting comparison, a gunshot tuning loop — and threw it away each time, while the category it covers produced eight user-found defects in the same session. Building it once as a kept harness was available the whole time and I did not take it.

**Study prompt — Topic 1 (paste into a fresh session):**
> In a Three.js game I shipped an arena that was not a closed volume — a flood fill from outside reached every spawn point — despite nine passing invariant tests over its geometry (every doorway wide enough, every nav envelope clear, every corridor convex, every sightline terminating inside its room). Three more defects that session had the same shape: a shadow frustum where only one of four containment axes was checked, two wall meshes occupying the same space with no test asking, and a crosshair that pointed somewhere different from the shot. Teach me: (1) the distinction between properties of parts and closure properties of an assembly, and how to tell which one a requirement is; (2) the standard failure mode where a suite of local invariants is green while a global one is false; (3) how this appears outside games — ledgers, auth paths, state machines, schemas; (4) a practical method for deriving the closure assertions a system needs, and how property-based testing expresses them; (5) canonical resources.

**Study prompt — Topic 2:**
> Same project. Three shadow defects with three unrelated causes, all looking identical from the outside ("that wall seems to be floating"): a shadow-camera extent hardcoded to a retired arena size; a directional light sitting 34 units out inside a 113-unit arena, so a whole district fell behind its own near plane; and `shadow.bias`, which three.js scales by `far - near`, silently doubling in world-space effect as the arena grew — I caused that one while fixing the second. Teach me: (1) how a shadow camera frames a scene and what each of extent/near/far/light-distance actually constrains; (2) what `bias` and `normalBias` offset, in what units, and why a caster's own lit faces are not in the depth map by default; (3) peter-panning vs acne as a single tradeoff; (4) the general habit for constants whose units are defined by another value — bound the derived quantity, not the literal; (5) canonical resources.

**Study prompt — Topic 3:**
> Same project: eight defects in one session were purely visual — shadow seams at wall bases, z-fighting between adjacent coloured walls, bullet decals rendering as hard black squares, a muzzle flash that was a visible hexagon. All eight were found by a human looking at the screen; none was visible to a 510-test suite, because nothing in it renders. I settled several by sampling the framebuffer directly (one column across a wall/floor junction: 76 luminance at the seam against 47 on the floor either side). Teach me: (1) how to assert properties of rendered output without brittle golden-image diffs; (2) which visual defect classes reduce to measurable numeric properties and which genuinely need human judgement; (3) how frame-to-frame instability (z-fighting) is detected automatically; (4) how teams structure a visual test layer so it does not become the flakiest thing they own; (5) canonical resources.

## Cumulative Model Update

**Before (08-06):** every significant defect lives where two *conventions* cross a boundary — sim yaw vs camera yaw, entity clock vs world clock, one event producer vs two, pass N's GPU state vs pass N+1's. Correctness there is relational, not local.

**After:** that still holds — and the boundary model turned out to be only half of it. The other half is **properties that no part owns**. Enclosure belongs to no wall. Frustum containment belongs to no caster. Non-overlap belongs to neither of the two meshes. Crosshair-shot agreement belongs to neither the camera nor the weapon. A codebase can be composed entirely of correct, well-tested parts and still be wrong, and no amount of per-part rigour will surface it — the assertion has to be written against the assembly.

Second update, about *observability* rather than correctness: this project can see its simulation in complete detail and cannot see its output at all. That asymmetry is exactly where the defects now concentrate — 8 of 13 this session — and it is a tooling gap, not a knowledge gap.

**Because:** thirteen defects, zero caught by 510 passing tests; four of them global properties of an assembly of valid parts, eight of them things only an eye could see.

## Next Session: One Thing To Do Differently

**Keep the measurement rig.** Promote the throwaway headless-Chromium harness into a permanent, small `test/visual/` layer that asserts **numeric properties of rendered pixels** — not image diffs — for the handful of things that have already broken: seam contrast across a wall/floor junction, frame-to-frame stability at a colour boundary, the on-screen size of an effect.

I built that rig three times this session and deleted it three times. In the same session, eight defects reached the user because nothing but a person could see them. The capability is proven — it settled the shadow-seam question with a number (76 → 49) after two rounds of confident wrong reasoning — and it currently costs about twenty minutes to rebuild and zero to keep.
